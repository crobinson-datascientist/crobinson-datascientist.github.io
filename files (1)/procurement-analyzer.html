Procurement Backlog Analyzer

View Live Dashboard ↗

Problem

The procurement team knew the backlog was growing but had no way to quantify it or explain it. Requisitions came in faster than purchase orders went out, and the gap widened month over month. "How bad is it?" had no answer beyond a raw count of open items, which meant nothing to anyone outside the team.

The more consequential gap was diagnostic. No one could distinguish backlog caused by buyer capacity from backlog stalled on the requester side — waiting on specs, budget approval, or vendor sign-off. Every escalation landed on procurement regardless of where the item was actually stuck. That made staffing conversations unproductive: leadership couldn't tell whether the team was under-resourced or whether half the queue was blocked on inputs the team didn't control.

Process

I built a Looker Studio dashboard on consolidated requisition and PO data, structured around one headline metric and three diagnostic breakdowns.

Intake versus output. Reqs received charted against POs produced by month. This is the simplest view on the dashboard and the one that reframed the conversation — the gap between the two bar series is the backlog, accumulating visibly.

The backlog analyzer. Rather than reporting a count, this calculates estimated work days to clear the queue: open POs divided by average daily throughput derived from the prior year's completed orders. The reasoning is that a count is an abstraction while a capacity figure is actionable — "eleven work days of committed work" tells leadership something a number like "193 open items" does not. Using a full prior year as the throughput baseline smooths seasonal variation rather than over-fitting to a recent good or bad month.

Three segmented views. By buyer, showing workload distribution and aging so imbalances are visible. By pending requester action, naming the requester, department, and the specific blocker. By entity, showing organizational concentration of open items.

Result

The days-to-clear metric gave leadership a concrete capacity number instead of an abstract count, which changed the character of staffing and prioritization discussions — they became conversations about a known quantity of committed work rather than arguments about whether the backlog was "bad."

Separating requester-blocked items from buyer-queued items redistributed accountability accurately. Requesters could see exactly what was waiting on them, and procurement stopped absorbing escalations for delays it didn't cause. Buyer-level aging data made workload imbalances visible and actionable rather than anecdotal.

A note on this demo. The linked dashboard is a recreation built for portfolio purposes. All buyer names, requester names, entities, and figures are fictional. No proprietary or employer-confidential data is represented. The metric definitions, segmentation logic, and throughput calculation reflect the original work.

AI Procurement Risk Framework for Public Agencies

PythonWeighted Decision Modeling GSA DataPolicy Analysis
Status: Planned. Scoping and source identification underway; no analysis conducted yet. This page describes the intended design.

Problem

Government agencies are procuring AI tools at increasing scale without standardized risk assessment. Existing procurement frameworks were written for software and services with predictable behavior — they don't account for model drift, training data provenance, automated decisions affecting individuals, or the vendor lock-in that comes from proprietary model weights.

The practical consequence is that contracting officers evaluating an AI procurement often have no structured basis for distinguishing a low-risk document-summarization tool from a system making eligibility determinations about citizens.

Intended approach

Develop a weighted scoring rubric and apply it to real government AI contracts using publicly available sources, including GSA schedules and published agency AI use case inventories. The rubric would evaluate each contract based on decision-making autonomy, the size of the affected population, the reversibility of potential harm, data sensitivity, vendor transparency, and the cost or difficulty of transitioning away from the system.

Output would be a tiered risk classification with a corresponding procurement checklist, specific questions and contract clauses appropriate to each tier, rather than a single one-size assessment.

This connects directly to my procurement background: the useful version of this framework isn't an academic taxonomy, it's something a contracting officer can actually work through before a solicitation goes out.

Open questions

The main methodological challenge is validation. A scoring rubric is a set of assumptions expressed as weights, and without an outcome variable to check against, there's no obvious way to demonstrate the weights are right rather than merely plausible. Options I'm considering include expert elicitation for weight-setting, sensitivity analysis across weighting schemes to identify which conclusions are robust, and back-testing against documented AI procurement failures.

I'd rather state that problem plainly now than present a rubric later as though the weights were derived rather than chosen.
